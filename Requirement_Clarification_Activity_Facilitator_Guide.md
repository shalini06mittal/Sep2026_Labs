## Purpose

This activity teaches fresh graduates why they must clarify vague requirements before starting work, instead of assuming details and diving straight in. Participants experience firsthand how an apparently "simple" request hides many unstated assumptions — and how asking the right questions up front produces a far better outcome than guessing.

## 1. Warm-Up Analogy: "Can You Order Some Food for the Group?"

Use this everyday scenario to introduce the idea before connecting it to work requirements. Someone in a group chat says:

> **Prompt:** "Can you order some food for the group?"

It sounds simple — but it is just as loaded as any vague work requirement. Walk participants through what happens if they assume instead of asking:

- "Some food" — pizza? Are you ordering variety, or one big thing everyone shares?
- "For the group" — how many people, exactly? Does that number include the person who said this, or people running late?
- Allergies/diet — nobody said anyone's allergic to shellfish or is vegetarian — do you already know that, or are you about to find out the hard way?
- Timing — is there an event with a start time, or is "whenever" fine?
- Payment — who's paying, and does "order" mean you're fronting the money and getting reimbursed?

If you just open a food delivery app and start picking items (the equivalent of "opening the diagramming tool and drawing boxes"), you'll produce an order — just not necessarily one that feeds the actual group, at the actual time, without someone going hungry because of an allergy nobody mentioned.

## 2. Activity Structure

Opening line for the activity:

> **Opening Line:** "Can you order food for the group?"

### How to run it

- One person plays the Requester and is given a hidden brief (headcount, allergies, budget, arrival time, dietary preferences, who's actually eating vs. just visiting).
- Everyone else has a limited number of questions they may ask before they must "place the order."
- The group compares their naive first guess against the informed second attempt (after asking questions).

### Alternate prompts (for variety across groups or rounds)

Rotate these if running multiple rounds or groups so participants don't repeat the same scenario:

- "Can you plan something for Friday night?" — (whose Friday, what budget, is it everyone or just some people, indoor/outdoor)
- "Book us a place to stay for the trip." — (how many rooms, how many nights, near what, whose budget)
- "Make a playlist for the road trip." — (whose taste wins, how long is the drive, any hard no's)
- "Can you grab groceries for the apartment?" — (whose stuff, shared vs. personal items, budget, dietary restrictions)

## 3. Main Worked Example: "Can You Plan Something for Friday Night?"

This is the core teaching example. Walk participants category by category, showing how many legitimate clarifying questions a single vague sentence can generate.

### 1. Clarifying the Basic Requirement

1. What does "something" mean?
2. Is this a social event, dinner, party, outing, movie, or something else?
3. What does "plan" mean here?
4. Do you want just an idea, or a complete plan?
5. Who is asking us to plan it?
6. Who is the plan for?
7. What is the objective of the evening?
8. Is there a specific reason for the event?
9. Is this a one-time event or something recurring?

### 2. Clarifying "Friday"

1. Which Friday do you mean?
2. Do you mean this coming Friday?
3. What date is that Friday?
4. Which time zone are we talking about?
5. Does the date matter because Friday could mean different dates depending on when the requirement is given?
6. Is the plan fixed for Friday, or could another day work?

> **💡 Teaching Point:** "Friday" is not necessarily a sufficient requirement. We should convert relative terms into specific dates.

### 3. Clarifying "Night"

1. What time should the evening start?
2. What time should it end?
3. What do you consider "night"?
4. Is 5 PM acceptable?
5. Is 7 PM acceptable?
6. Is 10 PM too late?
7. Do we need to be finished by a particular time?
8. Is there a curfew or deadline?
9. Does the duration matter?

### 4. People / Participants

1. How many people are attending?
2. Who is attending?
3. Are they friends, colleagues, family, or clients?
4. Are children attending?
5. Are elderly people attending?
6. Are there any accessibility requirements?
7. Does everyone know each other?
8. Do we need an activity that works for a large group?
9. Is attendance mandatory or optional?
10. Do we need to send invitations?
11. Do we need to track RSVPs?

### 5. Location

1. Where should this happen?
2. Is it indoors or outdoors?
3. Should it be at someone's home?
4. Should it be at a restaurant?
5. Should it be somewhere public?
6. How far are people willing to travel?
7. Is there a preferred neighborhood?
8. Does the location need parking?
9. Is public transportation important?
10. Should it be close to everyone's homes?
11. Should it be close to the office?
12. Is travel time a constraint?
13. Does the location need to be accessible?

### 6. Budget

1. What is the budget?
2. Is there a maximum budget?
3. Is the budget per person or total?
4. Who is paying?
5. Should everyone pay individually?
6. Is the budget flexible?
7. Does the budget include transportation?
8. Does it include food?
9. Does it include tickets?
10. Does it include taxes and service charges?
11. Are there any cost restrictions?

### 7. Preferences

1. What kind of activity do you prefer?
2. Is there a preferred type of food?
3. Are there cuisines we should avoid?
4. Does anyone have dietary restrictions?
5. Does anyone have allergies?
6. Are there activities people don't want to do?
7. Do you prefer something relaxing or adventurous?
8. Indoor or outdoor?
9. Casual or formal?
10. Quiet or lively?
11. Restaurant, entertainment, or activity?
12. Is there a preferred venue?
13. Are there places you definitely don't want?

### 8. Transportation

1. How will everyone get there?
2. Does anyone need transportation?
3. Should we arrange taxis/rides?
4. Is parking required?
5. Is public transportation available?
6. How long is the acceptable commute?
7. Does everyone need to return home together?
8. What time does transportation need to be arranged?

### 9. Reservations / Availability

1. Do we need a reservation?
2. Should we check availability first?
3. What happens if our preferred place is fully booked?
4. Do we need a backup option?
5. How many seats/tickets do we need?
6. When should the reservation be made?
7. Is there a cancellation policy we need to consider?

### 10. Constraints

1. Are there any hard constraints?
2. Are there any things we must have?
3. Are there things we must not have?
4. Is there a maximum travel distance?
5. Is there a maximum cost?
6. Is there a minimum number of participants?
7. Is the timing fixed?
8. Is the location fixed?
9. Are there organizational policies we need to follow?
10. Are there legal or safety requirements?
11. Is weather a consideration?

### 11. Expected Output

*This is a particularly important set of questions for fresh grads.*

1. What exactly do you expect me to deliver?
2. Do you want one plan or multiple options?
3. Do you want the plan in an email?
4. Do you want a schedule?
5. Do you want estimated costs?
6. Do you want restaurant/activity recommendations?
7. Do you want maps or directions?
8. Do you want reservations to be made?
9. Do you want me to contact the participants?
10. When do you need the plan?
11. Who needs to approve it?

## 4. The Most Important Questions

After students generate their own long list of questions, teach them to identify the high-value clarification questions — the small subset that unlocks most of the ambiguity. Instead of asking 30 questions immediately, a good graduate should open with:

> **Model Response:** "Sure. Before I plan it, could you clarify a few things?"

- Which Friday/date do you mean?
- What time should the evening start and end?
- Who is attending and how many people?
- What type of activity are you looking for?
- Where should it be?
- What is the budget?
- Are there any preferences or restrictions?
- What exactly would you like me to deliver — ideas, a detailed itinerary, or actual bookings?
- When do you need the plan finalized?
- Are there any non-negotiable requirements?

## 5. Additional Practice Scenarios

Use these as follow-up rounds once participants grasp the pattern, to show it applies equally to technical/work requirements, not just everyday planning.

### 5.1 "Add a login feature to the application."

Ask the group: "Is that any clearer?" — The answer is no. Prompt them to generate questions such as:

- Who can log in?
- Username/password?
- Email/password?
- SSO?
- Google login?
- MFA?
- Password reset?
- Account lockout?
- What happens after 5 failed attempts?
- What roles exist?
- What happens if the user enters invalid credentials?
- What should the error message say?
- Should sessions expire?
- How long should they remain logged in?
- What security requirements exist?

### 5.2 "Can you build a report for the customer?"

Ask participants: isn't this exactly the same problem as before? Break the sentence into its ambiguous terms:

**"Report" could mean:**

- PDF
- Excel
- Dashboard
- Web page
- Email
- PowerPoint

**"Customer" could mean:**

- Which customer?
- Which customers?
- Customer ID?
- Customer segment?

**"Data" could mean:**

- Which fields?
- Which date range?
- Real-time or historical?
- What filters?
- What calculations?

**"Build" could mean:**

- Prototype?
- Production-ready?
- API?
- UI?
- Database query?

## 6. Facilitation Notes

- Start with the food-ordering analogy — it's low-stakes and everyone relates to it immediately.
- Run the "Friday night" example as the deep-dive: let participants generate questions category by category before revealing the full list.
- Emphasize the "Most Important Questions" step — the goal is not to teach students to ask 100 questions, but to prioritize the handful that matter most.
- Close with the login-feature and customer-report scenarios to show the same discipline applies directly to technical/work requirements.
- Reinforce throughout: relative or vague terms ("Friday," "some food," "a report") must be converted into specific, unambiguous requirements before work begins.
